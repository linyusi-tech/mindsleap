**Author: Lincoln Wang | Founder & CEO, MindsLeap | Partner and CEO, Founders Space China | Founder, MindsLeap Founders AI Club**

This article is Lincoln's interpretation of [Applying Gemini 4 for large-scale codebase maintenance in Chromium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LVEfFyijQ2c), published by the official Google DeepMind channel on October 13, 2026.

Why can a change to a browser's bookmarks bar affect a seemingly unrelated feature? In large software systems, that is hardly unusual. Features accumulate, teams change, and dependencies become harder to understand. Users see a button; engineers may face two decades of tangled connections underneath it.

In the video, Google engineer Erik Chen uses Chromium as a concrete example. He describes an open-source project with approximately 40 million lines of code. After gaining early access to Gemini 4 Argon, he says he advanced more changes in two weeks than he would normally complete in several months.

This is a developer's account in an official vendor video, not an independently controlled experiment. Still, it illustrates a direction worth examining: AI coding is moving into the accumulated systems that companies find difficult to change.

## A Tangle Beneath the Bookmarks Bar

Beyond Chromium's core engine are features such as bookmarks, tabs, and downloads. Different teams added them at different times. Each solved a local need, while the connections between features gradually became more complex. Some dependencies are invisible in the interface.

Chen's team wants to separate these features into modules that can be developed and tested independently. This is refactoring: reorganizing internal structure while trying to preserve existing behavior, making future changes easier. Its value often becomes apparent only during later development, so urgent product requests can push it down the priority list.

Chen describes apparently unrelated features that are connected underneath. Many enterprise systems have the same problem. After years of changes to orders, inventory, billing, and customer management, a change in one area can affect another workflow. Maintenance slowly consumes the capacity to innovate, yet rarely attracts the attention of a new product launch.

## Models Used to Get Lost, Too

The team had previously tried other models. According to Chen, Chromium's scale made it difficult for AI agents to navigate effectively. Generated code did not meet expectations, and the process required considerable human supervision.

That explains the gap between writing code and maintaining a large system. Working on legacy software requires understanding existing tools, dependencies, and constraints, then checking whether old behavior survives an internal change. A well-written standalone function is not proof that a change belongs in a production codebase.

In the workflow he describes, the team gives the model a dependency-removal objective and memory files explaining how to use existing tools, then lets it work on multiple changes in parallel. He mentions launching 20 changes at a time. This describes concurrency in this particular case; it does not establish a twentyfold productivity gain for engineering in general.

## Twenty Changes, Backed by an Acceptance Process

Another detail matters more: Chen says each change typically runs through hundreds of thousands of tests, followed by review from two human engineers and millions of tests across newer and older devices.

AI output enters an established engineering process. Tests help detect behavioral regressions. Human reviewers continue to judge whether the implementation makes sense. Device testing checks behavior across environments. Together, these steps determine whether a change can be accepted.

Passing tests does not prove that software is free of defects. It does, however, provide inspectable results for parallel execution. The transferable lesson for business leaders is to give agents clear tasks and clear acceptance criteria. Permissions, review, and failure handling should develop alongside execution capacity.

## A Chance to Revisit Legacy Systems

Chen concludes by describing how the team can now reconsider where Chromium should go. In his account, the ability to get work done had been a limiting factor, and models make more improvements possible.

That brings to mind projects that never reach the top of an enterprise's priority list: removing duplicate data, cleaning up interfaces, completing documentation, and separating tightly coupled modules. Individually, they rarely seem as urgent as current growth targets. Collectively, they determine how quickly an organization can adapt.

For me, the business value of this kind of case is that it invites a fresh assessment of technical debt. If AI helps a team reduce maintenance costs, the benefit includes both hours saved and more room to change products and processes later. But that benefit must be demonstrated through real outcomes, not the volume of code generated.

## Start with a Small Part of the System and Define Acceptance

The vendor's case video does not provide complete error rates, project costs, or long-term maintenance data. We can treat it as a signal worth testing while keeping those limits in view.

For an enterprise with an established engineering team, a reasonable starting point is a clearly bounded maintenance task with mature testing conditions. Let the model participate, then examine rework, review time, and subsequent maintenance. For a company without a complete testing system, defining an acceptable result may be more valuable than rushing to expand automation.

AI gives teams more execution capacity. Turning it into a lasting organizational capability also requires deciding which changes matter, how to verify them, and who takes over when something goes wrong. The Chromium example brings these questions together and gives enterprise AI transformation a concrete engineering context.
